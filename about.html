Why product

Why I’m exploring product management

Writing code taught me how a product works on the inside. What draws me now is the thinking that has to happen before any code: which problem is worth solving, what the right solution looks like, which constraints matter, and, once it’s out, whether it actually made a difference.

Some of my PPFAS work already sits on that line. With the portals I built, the value was in what business teams could now run themselves, more than in the code behind them.

So the move towards product management is deliberate, and I intend to keep building: understanding engineering constraints first-hand is part of what I’d bring to the role. The engineering side

What an engineering background brings to product work

Good engineering decisions aren’t only about whether something works — they’re also about understanding what the evidence actually tells you. Four examples from my own work:

  • Designing with AI usage and infrastructure constraints in mind. SYNAPSE supports three AI providers, uses monthly token limits by plan, and allows users to bring their own API keys, which are stored encrypted.
  • Testing feasibility by building. SignBridge set out to show that turning speech into meaning, then into a sign sequence, then into visuals was technically feasible. The prototype takes a spoken video through each of those steps and plays the signs back in sync with the video. What it does not show is real sign coverage: only 2 of its 209 sign entries are real sign clips, and the rest are placeholder cards.
  • Not trusting a metric blindly. In SignBridge’s avatar research, a fix that stopped the avatar’s resting arm passing into its hip made the arms visibly correct, but raised the wrist trajectory error on the HELLO sign from 0.0354 to 0.0665, because that metric only measures the shape of the wrist’s path and has no concept of an arm passing through the body. I kept the fix and documented why.
  • Being specific about limits. SignBridge’s translations are AI-generated and haven’t been reviewed by Deaf or sign-language interpreters, and its case study says so. SYNAPSE’s case study is just as direct: it’s a prototype that isn’t deployed and has no automated tests.

Problem areas in my work

Workflows, payments and accessibility

Three problem areas show up in work I’ve built:

  • Internal workflows and what they cost. Internal tools can reduce a recurring cost or a dependency on another team. The PPFAS portals I built, described under “What I do today”, are my clearest example.
  • Payments. I built the PPFAS Gift investment platform and integrated it with a third-party payment gateway, including international payment support; I did not build the gateway itself. SYNAPSE includes Razorpay subscription billing, with checks that payment notifications are genuine; it has not been connected to live payments.
  • Accessibility beyond captions. Captions help many people, but they aren’t sign language, and spoken video isn’t automatically available in sign-language form. SignBridge is my research prototype for exploring that gap in Indian Sign Language.
